Cười Vui no. 20 (2014), put online for people who have never heard of it

A Vietnamese children's humour magazine, scanned page by page, described in English, partly transcribed, and used to test how far an AI can be trusted with its lettering.

1What is this magazine?

Cười Vui is Vietnamese for roughly “laugh and have fun”. According to the cover and the masthead printed inside, it was a weekly publication of the children's newspaper Nhi Đồng, listed as coming out on Fridays. This copy is issue no. 20, dated 16 May 2014, with a printed price of 4,000 đồng and an editorial office listed in Hanoi.

It is made for school-age readers: jokes and rhymes sent in by readers, short comic stories, a four-page comic strip, entertainment news, quizzes and prize contests, with cartoon characters on almost every page.

What is inside this issue

  • Lớp tôi (“My class”): a humorous rhyme sent in by a reader, and Văn phòng Bố Đa: readers' questions answered with jokes.
  • Gọi bằng… BỐN (“Call it… four”, the cover story) and Cảnh sát và những tình huống khó xử (“Police and awkward situations”): short jokes.
  • Entertainment pages on TV comedy and comedians, a poem about fishing, and Cười xả láng (“laugh out loud”): one-line dialogue jokes.
  • Sói Vin và Bíp Bíp: a four-page comic strip about a coyote and a fast-running bird.
  • Two pages of advertising for a milk brand's comic series, a reader contest with a prize-winners list, and a second contest.
  • Nhất quỷ nhì ma on the back cover: an eight-panel classroom comic.

2Purpose and audience

✎ DRAFT: reworded from your own message; rewrite in your own voice

We chose the Documentation focus. Our purpose is to make one issue of this magazine accessible to many more people: first of all, people who have no information about the magazine at all, and beyond them a universal audience. That is why this site is in English, describes every page, and puts a Vietnamese transcription and an AI check next to a sample of the pages.

Our second priority is to archive the issue as faithfully as we can, without damaging the original. Where the two priorities pull against each other, access comes first, but never at the cost of harming the paper copy.

Permission

✎ DRAFT: built from your message; add anything you did beyond this

Professor Daniel Ruelle approved the choice of this issue for the project. For the purposes of this assignment it is fine for the team to publish the pages on this website, and the issue is now 12 years old.

Privacy: what we hid, and what we left visible

The magazine prints readers' addresses, villages, schools and districts beside their names, and the prize-winners' list on page 18 prints full home addresses. On this site every one of those lines is covered with a plain black box: the locations of 16 named contributors and prize-winners across six spreads. The boxes are drawn over the picture. Nothing was repainted or reconstructed, and no AI was used to edit the images. Names stay visible. The unredacted scans are kept offline and are not published.

We left the publisher's own office addresses and phone numbers visible, because they are the organisation's contact details, not a private person's. We also left the owner's inscription on the front cover, because it shows who kept this copy.

Rights note. The pages contain work by other people: illustrators, photographers, advertisers and licensed cartoon characters. They are shown here at reduced size only as part of a university class project that documents this issue, and are not offered for reuse. If you hold rights to something shown here and want it taken down, please contact the team: 24khanh.t@vinuni.edu.vn.

5Sample transcription and the AI check

We transcribed a sample so that it could be read, searched and copied, and so that we could test an AI against it. Đinh Ngọc Đại typed a human “ground truth” from the page. He then had Claude (an AI) transcribe the same images and compared the two. The sample covers the Lớp tôi spread and the Thắc mắc joke.

6Three decisions that shaped this digital version

The full decision log is in our workbook. These three mattered most for what the digital version can and cannot tell you.

✎ DRAFT: built from your decision-log sheet; check every line is how you'd say it

1. Which scan became the master, and not pressing pages flat

What we decided
We used V3, the Apple Notes scan, as our master. We did not press pages flat to force them straight, and when cropping we cut along straight lines, even where that cropped away some of the page edge (mostly plain colour). The back cover is the exception: V3's had glare, so we replaced it with a cleaner photograph from our second set (V2), which is that page's master.
What we tried first
We began by testing the softbox setup that Professor Dan kindly set up for us. With it, the lighting was not consistent (it got dimmer towards the top and bottom of the page) and, most importantly, the cover pages had too much glare, which could not be fixed afterwards.
Why Notes
Notes has a convenient autoscan feature, which we wanted to test.
Why we did not press the pages flat
We wanted to limit any further damage to the copy, as it was old and quite thin.
What it changed or lost
The app cropped the pages and balanced the brightness automatically. We found this did not affect the clarity of the image, and we chose this scanning method for our master.
What we would do with more time
We would scan the pages much more carefully. Perhaps we would adjust the lighting until it was ideal for each page, instead of relying on the Notes correction.
Example
Our Notes scan had glare in the right corner of the back cover, which could not be edited out afterwards. Our V2 photograph, taken under the same lighting but without the Notes app's automatic brightness balancing, did not have it. We straightened that photograph with a four-cornered, straight-edged outline cut just inside the page edge, giving V5 (right). In V4 (centre) the same cover had come out stretched and with a patch of glare.
The trade-off: one could say the glary version was more true to life, as in bright lighting that could be how it looks.
✎ DRAFT: from your sheet and your answers; check it is how you'd say it

2. Cover every contributor's address with a plain black box

What we decided
On the public copies, the address, village, school and district lines of 16 named people are covered with solid black boxes. Names stay. We did not use AI to fill, blur or repaint anything.
Why
To protect the privacy of the people involved. The magazine is old and they may have moved, but we did not find it suitable to publish their addresses online.
What else we could have done
We could have left the addresses visible, which would have been the most accurate. We chose privacy instead. We could also have blurred them, but a blur might be reversible, so we covered them with solid black boxes.
What it changed or lost
A reader can no longer tell where these people were from, or possibly where they lived. What stays true is that these named people won these prizes.
Example
The prize-winners' list on page 18, shown in section 3: names and prizes remain; 13 address lines are blacked out.
✎ DRAFT: from your sheet and your answers; check it is how you'd say it

3. Scan everything, transcribe only a sample, and check the AI against a human

What we decided
We scanned the whole issue, but transcribed only the Lớp tôi spread and one joke from page 14. One teammate typed a ground truth by hand; Claude transcribed the same pages; the two were then compared with each other and with the page.
Why
The 4–10 page size guide was behind the decision. We picked the Lớp tôi spread and the Thắc mắc joke because we wanted to examine the page formatting and the short, somewhat childish nature of these texts.
What it changed or lost
Only the sample can be searched, copied or read by a screen reader. Everything else is a picture with a short description.
Example
The table in section 5. The human version was not perfect either: it left out a word, a speech bubble, and mis-typed a tone mark. Without the check against the page, those errors would have counted as “truth”.

7What we found

✎ DRAFT: arranged from your answers; change anything that isn't how you'd say it

1. The AI's mistakes were mostly in the diacritics

What bothered us most was the diacritics (the tone marks and other small marks on Vietnamese letters). Dust or a low-quality capture could affect them, but the AI got almost everything else right, so having it miss the diacritics was frustrating. The table in section 5 lists each case.

Evidence. In Anh Tú's answer the page has nịnh mẹ (“flatter mum”). Claude wrote ninh mẹ (“stew mum”), which changes the joke, and then added a note saying it was “as printed”. It also dropped the horn in gia sư and the bar on Đ in Điện, and misread the marks on vẻ and tử.

2. Don't blindly trust any single transcription, including the human one

Making the human “ground truth” also involved interpretive choices, so it can't be treated as the final word either.

Evidence. The human version left out the word em and a speech bubble, put an ellipsis one word early, and typed the wrong tone mark on NGUYỄN. Claude got all four right.

3. What the digital copy gives us, and what it cost

It is a copy that can be viewed anywhere, at any time, at no monetary cost. That came at the cost of the work behind it: scanning, processing, interpreting, programming, uploading and hosting.

8Limitations

✎ DRAFT: the two main limits are from your answers; check, and pick which of the other points to keep

What the images can't tell you

A reader might assume these images show the pages as they look under normal lighting, or under smooth, consistent lighting. They don't. The lighting was uneven, and the Notes app adjusted the brightness and crop for us, so colour and brightness here are only approximate.

What we can and can't say about the AI

From our sample we can say how well Claude could “see” diacritics and how well it knew how and where to space the text. We can't know how much it understood the jokes, or how it came to transcribe them the way it did.

Other limits

  • Only a sample is transcribed. The rest of the issue is images with descriptions, so it cannot be searched or copied.
  • Addresses are covered, so this site cannot show where contributors lived or studied.
  • The AI was tested on one spread and one joke: a hand-lettered rhyme and some printed jokes. We cannot say how it would do on the rest of the issue.
  • Two readings in the sample (vẻ in line 1 and tử in line 5 of the rhyme) rest on a hand-drawn tone mark, so we settled them by the page's own logo and the sense of the line, not by the mark alone.
  • The English descriptions are short guides, not translations of the magazine.
